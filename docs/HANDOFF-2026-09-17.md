**State:** Cachalot 0.3.0 plus six commits, `main` clean, 53 tests passing.
**Two sessions ran on this date.** Sections 1 to 9 are the first; sections 10 to 13 are the second and

**Machine:** Mac Studio M3 Ultra, 96 GiB unified memory, macOS 27.0.

## 1. The headline: decode is concurrency-starved, not bandwidth-starved

The previous handoff states that decode is bound by SSD bytes per token. That is half true, and the other half
changes what is worth doing next.

Measured with `benchmarks/decode_anatomy.py` at a 36 GiB budget, 512-token prompt, 64 decoded tokens:

| | ms per token | share |
|---|---|---|
| blocked inside the resident store | 140.1 | 51.5 % |
| attention, MoE kernels, head, Python | 132.1 | 48.5 % |
| total | 272 | |

60.7 misses per token, 990 MiB read per token, 6.06 ms per miss, **3.81 GB/s achieved**.

`benchmarks/expert_read_scaling.py` then reads random experts from the same bank through the real
`ExpertReader`, with no model loaded and nothing wired:

| concurrent loads | ms per expert | GB/s |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.69 | 5.75 |
| 2 | 2.02 | 7.65 |
| 8 | 1.99 | 7.79 |
| 16 | 2.08 | 7.46 |

The drive and the reader deliver 7.8 GB/s at the `io_workers=8` the runtime already uses. Decode gets 3.81.
The difference is not latency, bytes, the destination or the page cache — all four were tested and excluded
(section 4). It is **concurrency**: a layer discovers about 1.5 misses, issues them, and waits. Roughly 1.5
reads are in flight at any moment, and 15.5 MiB in 6.06 ms at that concurrency is exactly 3.8 GB/s. Meanwhile
the drive is idle for the 48.5 % of each token that is compute, and the GPU is idle for the 51.5 % that is
reads.

Useful bytes are 60.7 x 15.48 MiB = 940 MiB per token, which is 121 ms at 7.8 GB/s, against 132 ms of compute.
**If reads and compute overlapped fully, a token would cost about 140 ms instead of 272: near 7 tok/s, without
reading one byte less or changing any numerics.** That is the prize, and nothing in the previous handoff's
ranked list targets it.

The obstacle is that overlap requires knowing a layer's misses before that layer runs, and the only mechanism
for that is routing prediction, whose precision collapses with lead time (section 3).

## 2. Engram on the internal SSD (old lever 7.1) is a null result

Committed as `287c1ca`.

The 3-bit Engram table inside the expert bank has identical row counts to the shipped FP8 table, layer for
layer (384,006,168 at layer 1 and 384,016,682 at layer 14), so row ids carry across unchanged. Its rows are
128 B in three regions against 264 B in two. On cold ids, 12,288 unique rows per layer, the shape of a
512-token prefill (`benchmarks/engram_rows_bench.py`):

| | per 512-token prefill |
|---|---|
| shipped FP8, USB drive | 1436 ms |
| affine 3-bit, internal SSD | 590 ms |

2.44x faster, and worth nothing. `benchmarks/engram_prefill_share.py` instruments a real prefill from outside
and shows the background prefetch already hides all of it:

    512-token prefill, 24.16 s: layer 1 read 629 ms starting 4 ms in, needed at 815 ms;
                                layer 14 read 1278 ms starting 6 ms in, needed at 8725 ms.
                                Blocked 0.0 ms, dequantization 10.2 ms.
    32-token prefill,  7.60 s:  blocked 0.0 ms, dequantization 1.5 ms.

Both layers' rows arrive before the layer that needs them, at both prompt sizes. Note also that the 3-bit row
costs three preads against two, so on a warm page cache it is *slower* (355 ms against 236 ms).

Moving Engram to the internal SSD is still worth doing one day as a robustness change, because it would retire
the dependency on the X10Pro. It is not a performance lever and it is not worth the Engram numerics risk.

## 3. Prediction knobs cannot buy the overlap (null result)

`CACHALOT_PREDICT_AHEAD` and `CACHALOT_PREDICT_TOPK` already exist, so this was pure A/B. All arms at 36 GiB,
512-token prompt, 64 decoded tokens.

Depth, one run each:

| arm | tok/s | MiB read/token | precision | wasted loads/token |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ahead 1, topk 3 (default) | 3.67 | 990 | 76 % | 6.4 |

| ahead 3 | 3.81 | 1633 | 31 % | 49.8 |

## 4. What is *not* the cause of the 6.06 ms miss

Each of these was measured and excluded, so do not re-test them:

- **The drive.** 7.8-8.7 GB/s at 8 concurrent loads on cold ids.
- **The destination.** Wired MLX slot buffers are faster than bytearrays, 1.60 ms against 1.79.
- **The GIL, or MLX running concurrently.** A background thread doing 4096x4096 bf16 matmuls costs the reader
  1.54 ms to 1.62 ms.
- **The page cache.** 1.77-1.83 ms per expert with it on, 1.78-1.94 with `F_NOCACHE`. In the runtime,
  `CACHALOT_PAGE_CACHE=1` is still worth having: 3.67 tok/s against 3.56 with it off.
- **The piece pool.** Nine scattered pieces per expert, eight of them submitted to a shared 16-thread pool,
  looked like an obvious convoy. It is not: the standalone reader runs the same code at 8 loaders and reaches
  7.8 GB/s.

## 5. Segmented LRU: opt-in, and budget-dependent

Committed as `6c5fb61`. `CACHALOT_EVICT=slru`, `CACHALOT_SLRU_PROTECTED` default 0.8. **LRU remains the
default.**

The offline replay (`benchmarks/simulate_policies.py`, now parameterized by expert size) reproduces
`docs/performance.md` section 3 exactly with FP4 bytes, so it is faithful. On the 3-bit bank at 44 GiB it
predicts decode hit rates of LRU 77.2 %, frequency+decay 77.9 %, segmented LRU 78.7 %. That settles the
contradiction recorded in section 7.2 of the previous handoff: the "39 % against 31 %" figure is not
reproducible and the "1 to 2 points" note is correct.

In the runtime, six interleaved pairs at 36 GiB, run in both orders:

    lru   3.56 3.60 3.61 3.59 3.61 3.50   mean 3.578 tok/s, 64.0 misses/token, 73.3 % hit
    slru  3.63 3.67 3.71 3.70 3.68 3.69   mean 3.680 tok/s, 63.8 misses/token, 73.4 % hit

Segmented LRU wins all six pairs, +2.9 %, while the hit rate barely moves — so the gain is not the one the
replay predicted, and its mechanism is not established.

At 44 GiB, the budget interactive chat actually uses, it reverses:

    lru   3.84 3.81 3.87 3.72   77.0 % hit, 55.2 misses/token
    slru       3.81 3.79 3.64   76.4 % hit, 56.5 misses/token

It costs 0.6 points of hit rate there, and a smaller protected share does not recover it (0.5 gives 76.3 % and
3.56 tok/s). Protecting twice-used experts helps while probation still holds most of the resident set and hurts
once the protected segment crowds out the churn LRU handles well.

## 6. Levers, re-ranked (superseded — see section 13)

This ranking stood at the end of the first session. The second session measured items 1 and 4 and closed
both; section 13 is the current list. It is kept here because sections 10 to 12 are written against it.

1. **Read/compute overlap.** The only lever with a large ceiling (272 ms per token against about 140 ms if
   overlap were perfect). Knob-level speculation is exhausted; this needs a mechanism that knows a layer's
   experts earlier without a second approximation. Ideas not yet tested: predicting from the *post-attention*
   hidden state of the same layer rather than the previous layer's input, which trades lead time for accuracy;
   or a continuous background warmer that fills idle bandwidth with globally hot non-resident experts instead
   of per-layer guesses.
2. **Fewer bytes per expert** (old 7.5). Unchanged: a Q2-class bank would cut bytes and raise residency at
   once. Still blocked on GGUF k-quants that MLX cannot read.
3. **Startup hotlist preload** (old 7.4). Unchanged, helps the first prompt of a session.
4. **`F_RDAHEAD 0`** (old 7.6). Untested, cheap.
5. **Engram to internal SSD**, as robustness only (section 2).
6. **Eviction policy.** Closed. Section 5 is the final word: 1.5 points on paper, nothing or worse in the
   runtime at the production budget.

## 8. Operating note added this session

Benchmarks must not run back to back. macOS parks a finished run's memory on the inactive list, and
`guarded_run.sh`'s preflight counts it as reclaimable while a naive check does not; two arms started in
immediate succession produced a 1.32 tok/s outlier and two preflight refusals. Every A/B in this document ran
through a gate that waits for free plus inactive plus speculative pages to exceed the planned wired set, and
for pressure level 1, before starting the next arm.

The guardian also killed two runs, both correctly. In each case an unrelated `~/.hermes/node` process grew to
roughly 16-50 GiB while the benchmark held its wired set, swap grew past 2 GB and pressure reached level 2.
This is the same class of situation that panicked the machine on 2026-09-15. At a 44 GiB budget this machine
has no margin for another large process.

## 9. A wrapped copy-paste silently drops the expert bank

On 2026-09-17 three chat sessions decoded at 0.90, 1.05 and 1.30 tok/s where the same configuration had done
4.3-5.6 the day before. Nothing had regressed. The documented one-liner wrapped across five terminal lines, and
zsh executed them as five commands: a bare `VAR=value` on its own line sets a shell variable, which is never
exported to a child process, so only `PYTHONPATH` (which sat on the same line as the interpreter) reached the
runtime. `--temperature 0.6` landed on its own line and produced `zsh: command not found: --temperature`.

Without `CACHALOT_EXPERT_BANK` the runtime serves routed experts from `CACHALOT_MODEL_PATH`, and without that
too, `config.default_model_path()` discovers the FP4 checkpoint by scanning `/Volumes`. The sessions therefore
ran FP4 experts over USB at 1.0 GB/s, which is exactly what one tok/s looks like.

How to recognise it, from the session's own output:

- the `expert bank: … (affine, 3-bit, 14.77 MiB/expert, 15360 experts)` line is **absent**;
- `ready in … (expert budget B GiB, wired W GiB)` shows `W = B + 16.2`, the auto formula, rather than the
  `CACHALOT_MLX_WIRED_LIMIT_GIB` that was passed;
- in `/stats`, `resident_bytes / resident_experts` is 18,800,640 (FP4) instead of 15,482,880 (3-bit).

`scripts/chat.sh [budget_gib] [wired_gib]` now launches the qualified configuration and cannot be split by a
terminal. It also refuses to start beside a live runtime and checks that both paths exist.

# Second session, 2026-09-17

Three commits, no throughput change, three levers closed and the ranking inverted. Everything below was
measured at a 36 GiB budget with a 512-token prompt and 64 decoded tokens, which is the configuration
sections 1 and 3 use, so the numbers are directly comparable to them.

## 10. What decode actually blocks on

Section 1 attributes decode's 3.81 GB/s to concurrency, and derives it from a mean of 6.06 ms per expert
read. That mean comes from the resident store's `ssd_read_seconds`, which records two different things
together: demand misses, read on the `expert-load` pool while the main thread waits, and speculative
prediction loads, read on the `expert-predict` pool while the GPU computes and blocking nothing.

`benchmarks/decode_anatomy.py` now separates them by the thread that performed each read, and intersects the
read intervals with the intervals during which the main thread was actually blocked inside the store.

    decode 64 tokens: 17.60 s = 3.64 tok/s (275 ms/token)
      expert hit rate 73.4% | 63.9 misses/token | 1030 MiB read/token

      demand    41.9/token | mean 5.28 ms, p50 5.19, p90 8.14, max 21.25
      predict   27.7/token | mean 5.43 ms, p50 5.33, p90 9.00, max 17.64
      drive busy 10.72 s of 17.60 s decode (60.9%); 2.25 reads in flight while busy

      blocked total            9.68 s = 151.3 ms/token (53.0% of decode)
      a demand read in flight  9.35 s = 146.1 ms/token (96.6% of blocked)
      only a predicted read    0.21 s =   3.3 ms/token ( 2.2% of blocked)
      no read outstanding      0.12 s =   1.9 ms/token ( 1.2% of blocked)

Four things follow, and they matter more than the throughput number.

**There is no per-read latency tax.** Demand reads and speculative reads take the same time, 5.28 ms against
5.43 ms. Whatever inflates a read inflates both equally, so the piece pool is not head-of-line blocking demand
reads behind speculative ones, and the store's own bookkeeping is not on the read path. Do not look for a
demand-read penalty; there is not one.

**The comparison that suggested otherwise was invalid.** `benchmarks/expert_read_scaling.py` reports amortized
throughput time, not per-read latency: its "2.02 ms per expert" at two concurrent loads is 7.65 GB/s aggregate,
which is an individual read latency near 4 ms. Against that curve the runtime's 5.28 ms at 2.25 in flight is
roughly what the drive gives, not a 2x anomaly.

**Store overhead is negligible.** 1.9 ms per token is spent blocked with no read outstanding at all — slot
acquisition, eviction and locks together. Eviction policy work cannot pay; this is an independent confirmation
of section 5.

**Prediction timing is already fine, and coverage is the whole problem.** Only 2.2 % of blocked time is spent
waiting on a load that was predicted correctly but issued too late. 96.6 % is spent on misses that were never
predicted at all. Section 1's diagnosis — that overlap requires knowing a layer's misses earlier — is right
about the mechanism and wrong about which half is binding: the predictions the runtime makes arrive in time,
there are simply not enough of them.

## 11. Prediction from an earlier activation is free, and useless

Section 6 proposed predicting from "the post-attention hidden state of the same layer rather than the previous
layer's input". The code already does exactly that. `moe_layer_forward` receives `ffn_input`, which is the
post-attention normalized hidden state of its own layer (`block_sliding_window.py`, the `hc_pre_norm_decode`
call feeding the MoE). That idea is not an available lever; it describes the status quo.

The opposite direction is available and was measured. `benchmarks/predict_routing.py` now scores two sources
against the same actual routing: "late", layer L+k's router applied to the post-attention activation layer L's
own router uses, which is what the runtime does; and "early", the same router applied to layer L's block input,
the residual stream before attention L, normalized with layer L+k's FFN norm weight. The early activation is
worse but is available one attention sooner, worth about 2.9 ms of lead against a 5.3 ms read.

Recall on the experts that actually missed the cache, 48 decoded tokens after a 256-token prompt:

| source, layer L+1 | top-3 | top-6 | top-12 | top-24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| late (post-attention, today) | 35.5 % | 62.3 % | 81.0 % | 90.0 % |
| early (block input) | 35.7 % | 58.3 % | 75.5 % | 85.8 % |

At top-3 the earlier activation costs nothing at all, and it only begins to cost above top-6. As a cross-check,
35.5 % of 60.2 misses is 21.4 predicted-and-used per token, against the 21.9 that `decode_anatomy.py` measures
independently in the runtime.

It is still not worth building, because section 10 bounds the prize at 3.3 ms per token, or 1.2 %. Both halves
of the idea are now measured and closed: the accuracy is available, and the timing it would buy is not what
decode is losing.

## 12. `F_RDAHEAD 0` is reachable now, and worth nothing

Section 7.6 of the 2026-09-16 handoff lists this as untested. It was worse than untested: `ExpertReader._fd`
set `F_RDAHEAD 0` only inside the branch that also sets `F_NOCACHE`, so the shipped `CACHALOT_PAGE_CACHE=1`
configuration never reached it and kernel readahead stayed on for every shard descriptor.

`CACHALOT_RDAHEAD=0` now turns readahead off with the page cache still enabled. Defaults are unchanged. Four
interleaved arms, run in the order on / off / off / on with the memory gate between them:

| arm | tok/s | MiB/token | ms per miss | drive busy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| readahead on (default) | 3.58 / 3.64 | 1029 / 1030 | 6.05 / 5.90 | 61.3 % / 60.9 % |
| `CACHALOT_RDAHEAD=0` | 3.57 / 3.63 | 1029 / 1030 | 6.05 / 5.86 | 61.4 % / 60.7 % |

A null result. The spread within an arm is larger than the difference between arms, and bytes, per-miss latency
and busy fraction are all unmoved. The plausible mechanism — nine scattered pieces per expert, so pages read
ahead of a piece belong to experts nobody wants — is real but evidently costs nothing measurable on this drive.
The knob is kept, defaulted to today's behaviour, in the same spirit as `CACHALOT_EVICT=slru`.

## 13. Levers, re-ranked again

The arithmetic that drives this ranking, all per decoded token at a 36 GiB budget:

    useful bytes            63.9 misses x 15.48 MiB   = 988 MiB
      at 6.25 GB/s, what the runtime achieves         = 170 ms
      at 7.79 GB/s, what the drive delivers           = 133 ms
    compute (attention, MoE, head, Python)            = 134 ms
    measured                                          = 275 ms

**Even with perfect, waste-free prediction the drive floor equals or exceeds compute.** Overlap cannot take
decode below roughly 6.2 tok/s at this bank size, and reaching even that needs a prediction mechanism that does
not exist. Reading fewer bytes lowers the floor directly, and because a smaller expert also raises residency at
a fixed budget, it cuts the miss count that sets the floor in the first place. That is now the top lever on
measured grounds rather than by assumption.

Widening prediction is not the way to close the coverage gap, and the reason is arithmetic rather than
empirical. Each extra predicted expert that is not resident is a read, so recall and bytes rise together:

| width | miss recall | bytes/token | drive-time floor | measured |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| top-3 (default) | 35.5 % | 1028 MiB | 177 ms | 275 ms |
| top-6 | 62.3 % | 1357 MiB | 233 ms | 260 ms |
| top-12 | 81.0 % | ~2300 MiB | ~396 ms | not run |

Widening overlaps better — top-6 runs at 89 % of its own floor against top-3's 62 % — and raises the floor
faster than it closes the gap. The two settle near a saddle, which is the full explanation of section 3's
+1.6 % for 37 % more bytes. Do not re-run the width sweep.

1. **Fewer bytes per expert** (was 2). Now first, on the arithmetic above. A Q2-class bank lowers the drive
   floor and raises residency at once. Still blocked on GGUF k-quants that MLX cannot read; the mixed-precision
   recipe in section 7.5 of the 2026-09-16 handoff is the quality trick to copy.
2. **Raising achieved bandwidth from 6.25 toward 7.79 GB/s.** The runtime gets about 81 % of what the drive
   delivers at the same concurrency. Worth roughly 37 ms per token if fully closed, and untouched by this
   session. Readahead is excluded (section 12); the page cache under a 60 GiB wired set is not, because every
   test of it in section 4 ran with nothing wired.
3. **Startup hotlist preload.** Unchanged, helps the first prompt of a session.
4. **Engram to internal SSD**, as robustness only (section 2).
5. **Read/compute overlap.** Closed as a knob-level lever, and bounded as an architectural one: ceiling about
   6.2 tok/s, 96.6 % of the blocked time is uncovered misses, and both routes to covering them — more width
   (this section) and more lead (section 11) — are measured and rejected.
6. **`F_RDAHEAD 0`.** Closed, null (section 12).
7. **Eviction policy.** Closed (section 5), and independently confirmed by the 1.9 ms per token of total store
   overhead in section 10.

## 15. The page cache under a large wired set is a null, and the drive is slower than recorded

Section 13 ranked "closing the gap between the 6.25 GB/s the runtime achieves and the 7.79 GB/s the drive
delivers" second, and named the page cache under a large wired set as the one candidate never tested under
realistic conditions: every measurement in section 4 ran on an idle machine with nothing wired, while decode
holds about 57 GiB wired at a 36 GiB budget. Wired pages can be neither compressed nor evicted, so with the
page cache enabled the kernel must reclaim a page for every page it reads, and that cost cannot appear on an
empty machine.

`benchmarks/expert_read_scaling.py --wire-gib N` allocates and wires N GiB of MLX buffers before reading, with
a heartbeat thread to hold them, and reads experts through the real `ExpertReader`. The full 2x2, every arm on
a disjoint set of 512 cold experts, at 2 and 8 concurrent loaders, run interleaved:

| | page cache off (F_NOCACHE) | page cache on |
|---|---|---|
| nothing wired | 6.75 / 6.81, 6.74 / 6.80 | 6.72 / 6.76 |
| 55 GiB wired | 6.69 / 6.79 | 6.62 / 6.64, 6.70 / 6.81 |

Every arm falls between 6.62 and 6.81 GB/s, a spread of 2.8 %. **Both variables are null.** Holding 55 GiB
wired costs nothing measurable, and the page cache neither helps nor hurts the bandwidth the drive delivers on
cold experts. The reclaim hypothesis is wrong.

### 15.1 Three measurement traps that produced false numbers first

These cost two full matrices before the table above was trustworthy, and any future probe here will hit them.

**`F_NOCACHE` does not reliably keep expert reads out of the page cache.** Reading the same 64 experts three
times through a reader with `bypass_page_cache=True`:

    pass 1: 178.5 ms   5.55 GB/s
    pass 2: 109.3 ms   9.07 GB/s
    pass 3: 109.3 ms   9.07 GB/s

Pass 1 is the drive's true single-stream rate. Passes 2 and 3 are partly served from memory despite the flag.
With the page cache on the same repeat reaches 21.5 GB/s, which is RAM outright. `CACHALOT_PAGE_CACHE=0`
therefore does not mean what its name suggests, and the note in the 2026-09-16 material that F_NOCACHE does not
evict pages already cached understates it: it does not reliably stop them being cached in the first place.

**An arm is only valid on experts nothing has read yet.** The first attempt at this matrix used the same
`--seed` for every arm, so all six processes read the same 512 experts and every arm after the first measured
the page cache. It reported 17 to 64 GB/s, which is physically impossible for this drive and is the tell.
`--expert-offset` now slices one seeded shuffle disjointly so each arm gets cold experts by construction.

**Residual warmth survives across processes.** The second attempt used disjoint offsets but its two unwired
arms, which ran first, read experts the discarded first matrix had already touched. They reported 7.17 to
7.38 GB/s against 6.57 to 6.86 for the wired arms that followed, which looked exactly like a 7 % cost of
wiring memory. It was not: once every arm reads genuinely cold experts, the unwired arms sit inside the wired
range. Do not trust a wired-versus-unwired comparison whose arms are not both cold and interleaved.

### 15.2 What this does to the numbers in sections 10 and 13

**The drive is slower than this document records.** Section 4's "7.8-8.7 GB/s at 8 concurrent loads on cold
ids" and section 1's 7.65-7.79 GB/s scaling table are not reproducible on genuinely cold experts; both were
measured with `F_NOCACHE` set and are, on the evidence above, partly page-cache hits. The drive delivers
**6.6 to 6.8 GB/s** on cold random experts at 2 to 8 concurrent loaders, and 5.55 to 5.75 GB/s single-stream.

That changes what decode's inefficiency consists of. While the drive is busy the runtime moves 6.25 GB/s, which
is **93 % of the 6.7 GB/s actually available**, not 81 % of a 7.79 that does not exist. There is no meaningful
bandwidth left to recover at a given concurrency, and section 13's second lever largely evaporates: the gap is
worth about 11 ms per token, not 37.

The floor arithmetic moves with it:

    useful bytes   63.9 misses x 15.48 MiB       = 988 MiB/token
      at 6.7 GB/s, what the drive actually gives = 155 ms
    compute                                      = 134 ms

Perfect waste-free overlap therefore bottoms out near **155 ms per token, about 6.4 tok/s**, rather than the
140 ms and 7 tok/s section 1 projected. Every conclusion those sections draw survives, and each is now
stronger, because the drive's ceiling is lower than assumed while the compute side is unchanged: the system is
bytes-bound by a wider margin, and reading fewer bytes per expert is further ahead of everything else.

### 15.3 Revised ranking

1. **Fewer bytes per expert.** Unchanged and further ahead. It is the only lever that moves the 155 ms floor.
2. **Read/compute overlap.** Ceiling now about 6.4 tok/s, still requiring a prediction mechanism that does not
   exist (sections 10, 11, 13).
3. **Startup hotlist preload.** Unchanged, helps the first prompt of a session.
4. **Engram to internal SSD**, as robustness only (section 2).
5. **Closing the achieved-bandwidth gap.** Closed. 93 % of the drive is already achieved while reading.
6. **Page cache under a large wired set.** Closed, null (this section). `CACHALOT_PAGE_CACHE=1` stays on: it
   costs nothing here, and section 4's runtime A/B still shows it slightly ahead from genuine repeat hits.
7. **`F_RDAHEAD 0`**, **eviction policy**. Closed (sections 12, 5).

# Third session, 2026-09-17

Section 15.3 ranked reading fewer bytes per expert first and alone: it is the only lever that moves the 155 ms
drive floor, and it raises residency at a fixed budget at the same time. The 2026-09-16 handoff recorded that
lever as blocked, because ds4's Q2-class bank is GGUF with llama.cpp k-quants that MLX cannot read. It is not
blocked. The bank can be built here, from the FP4 checkpoint, in the layout the runtime already reads, and the
result is **smaller and better than the 3-bit bank the project has been running on**.

## 16. A 2-bit bank beats the 3-bit one, and the reason is the quantizer's fit

### 16.1 MLX's affine fit wastes a level at 2 bits

`mx.quantize` does not fit the affine grid to the group's range. Measured by quantizing known groups and
reading back the scales and biases it produces, its fit is max-abs symmetric:

    scale = +-max|w| / 2**(bits-1)        bias = -2**(bits-1) * scale

so the representable values are `scale * (q - 2**(bits-1))`. At 4 bits that costs little. At 2 bits the four
levels are `{-2, -1, 0, +1} * scale`: one level sits where the data never goes, and one side of the
distribution is clipped at half its range, which is why the sign of `scale` flips from group to group — the
fit puts the longer tail on the side that has three levels.

The stored format is only `w ~= scale * q + bias` per group, and `mx.dequantize` and `mx.quantized_matmul` take
the scales and biases as ordinary arrays, so **a better fit needs no runtime change at all** — only a packer.
`benchmarks/quant_affine.py` has both:

- `pack_2bit`, sixteen values per `uint32`, least-significant field first. `tests/test_quant_affine.py` pins
  that layout against `mx.quantize` itself, which is the part that must be exactly right: a bank built with it
  is read back by MLX's own kernels.
- `fit_search`, a per-group grid over shrunk range ends, keeping the lowest-squared-error `(scale, bias)`. It
  trades a little clipping for a finer step. The grid is 5x5: thirteen shrinks per end cost 241 ms per expert
  tensor against 37 ms for five and buy 0.0005 of relative weight error, and the gate quantizes some 20,000
  experts per run, so the difference is hours.
- `fit_minmax`, the textbook fit, which is **worse than MLX's** at 2 bits (output error 0.916 against 0.661):
  with four levels, clipping the outliers beats spanning them.

### 16.2 The gate: 2 bits at group 128 is better than 3 bits at group 64

`benchmarks/nll_expert_precision.py --experts requant` re-quantizes the FP4 experts on the fly at a chosen
width, group and fit, and scores them through the same dense fp32 reference math as the `fp4` and `oq3e` arms.
All arms are 160 teacher-forced tokens against the stored FP4 reference of 2.3004 nats, at a 24 GiB budget
(36 GiB failed the guard's preflight with 8 GiB already wired by other applications):

| arm | MiB/expert | bytes vs oQ3e | mean NLL | vs FP4 | perplexity | top-1 | greedy vs FP4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP4, dense reference math | 17.93 | 121 % | 2.3004 | — | 9.978 | 53.1 % | — |
| oQ3e 3-bit g64, calibrated | 14.77 | 100 % | 2.3398 | +0.0394 | 10.379 | 52.5 % | — |
| **our 3-bit g64, `mx.quantize`** | 14.77 | 100 % | **2.3097** | +0.0093 | 10.071 | 51.9 % | 86.2 % |
| **our 2-bit g64, `fit_search`** | 10.55 | 71.4 % | **2.3207** | +0.0203 | 10.183 | 46.9 % | 79.4 % |
| **our 2-bit g128, `fit_search`** | 9.49 | 64.3 % | **2.3234** | +0.0230 | 10.210 | 46.9 % | 75.0 % |
| our 2-bit g64, `mx.quantize` | 10.55 | 71.4 % | 2.3395 | +0.0391 | 10.376 | 50.6 % | 76.2 % |
| our 2-bit g128, `mx.quantize` | 9.49 | 64.3 % | 17.5460 | +15.2456 | 4.2e7 | 0.6 % | 1.9 % |

Three things come out of that table.

**Two bits at group 128, fitted properly, is 0.016 nats better than the 3-bit bank in use, at 64.3 % of its
bytes.** That is the byte lever, won, with quality to spare rather than quality spent.

**The fit is what buys it.** The same format with MLX's own fit is 0.019 nats worse at group 64, and at group
128 it does not merely degrade, it destroys the model: 17.5 nats, 0.6 % top-1, greedy agreement 3 tokens in
160. Fewer unique experts were even touched in that run (8,893 against 16,725), because routing had collapsed.
The cliff is the fit, not the group size — the same group size with `fit_search` scores 2.3234.

**Imatrix calibration is not what the 2026-09-16 handoff assumed.** Re-quantizing FP4 to 3 bits with plain
`mx.quantize` scores 0.030 nats *better* than oQ3e's calibrated 3-bit download at identical size. Whatever the
download's calibration bought, it is behind what is reachable here with no calibration at all. Section 7.5 of
the 2026-09-16 handoff named their mixed-precision recipe as "the quality trick to copy": it is not needed.

### 16.3 The cheap screen ranks formats, and must not be trusted across quantizers

`benchmarks/expert_requant_error.py` reads FP4 experts, re-quantizes them and reports the relative error of the
routed-expert output on random unit inputs. It costs seconds where the gate costs an hour, and over 40 layers
and two experts each it gives:

| format | MiB/expert | mean w-err | mean y-err |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-bit g64, `mx.quantize` | 14.77 | 0.2110 | 0.3517 |
| 3-bit g64, oQ3e calibrated | 14.77 | 0.1770 | 0.2958 |
| 2-bit g32, `mx.quantize` | 12.66 | 0.3784 | 0.5977 |
| 2-bit g64, `mx.quantize` | 10.55 | 0.4147 | 0.6544 |
| 2-bit g64, `fit_minmax` | 10.55 | 0.4572 | 0.9158 |
| 2-bit g64, `fit_search` | 10.55 | 0.3393 | 0.5467 |
| 2-bit g128, `fit_search` | 9.49 | 0.3690 | 0.5987 |

Within one quantizer the screen is useful and it picked the right format. **Across quantizers it is wrong**:
it ranks the calibrated download ahead of our naive 3-bit on both metrics, 0.296 against 0.352, and the gate
puts the naive one ahead by 0.030 nats. Use the screen to choose which formats deserve an NLL run and never
as a substitute for one.

It also settled two design questions before any bank was built. Error is spread evenly over layers (0.63 to
0.69 at 2-bit g64, a 5 % spread, with no gradient from layer 0 to 39) and evenly over the three projections
(0.276 for w1, 0.309 for w2, 0.276 for w3, quadrature-summing to the 0.487 the three together produce), so
neither a per-layer nor a per-projection mixed bank is indicated. Raising w2 to 3 bits buys 12 % of error for
13 % more bytes. A uniform bank is also the only kind `storage/index.py` accepts: it raises on mixed expert
quantization by design, and nothing needed to change there.

### 16.4 Cost of a gate run, and the trap in it

Each gate arm re-quantizes about 20,000 experts, not 15,360, because the 8 GiB packed cache evicts and some
experts are quantized more than once. At `mx.quantize` speed (19 ms per expert) an arm is 540 s. With the
13x13 search grid (720 ms per expert) the first 2-bit arm was still running at the guard's 5400 s timeout and
was killed. The 5x5 grid brings it to 125 ms and an arm to about 2200 s. If a future arm needs a finer fit,
raise the timeout rather than the grid, or build the bank and gate it with `--experts runtime` instead.

## 17. The bank

`benchmarks/build_affine_bank.py` writes a bank in the same stacked affine layout the oQ3e download uses, so
adopting it is `CACHALOT_EXPERT_BANK` and nothing else:

- one shard per layer, nine stacked tensors, written row by row with `pwrite` into a preallocated file, so the
  builder holds one expert at a time rather than a 4 GiB layer;
- it refuses to start unless the space it needs plus an 8 GiB margin is free;
- it skips shards a previous run finished, so an interrupted build resumes by re-running the same command;
- it writes `config.json` with the `omlx_deepseek_v41.quantized_modules` spec the runtime's
  `detect_expert_bank` reads, and a `cachalot_bank` block recording what it was built from;
- `--verify N` reads sampled experts back through the real index and compares them byte for byte against a
  fresh quantization of the source.

The bank being built is **2-bit group 128 with the searched fit**: 9,953,280 B per expert, 142.4 GiB, chosen
over group 64 because 0.0027 nats is cheap for 10 % fewer bytes.

### 17.1 The internal 3-bit bank was deleted to make room

the 2026-09-15 panic. The internal copy of the oQ3e bank was therefore deleted, after verifying that the
X10Pro copy can restore it: 65 shards present, every safetensors header consistent with its file's size, the
expert index builds 15,360 entries from it, and shard 30 of 65 is byte-identical to the internal copy. Free
space went from 170 to 495 GiB. Restoring it is a 331 GB copy from the X10Pro at 1 GB/s.

| 3-bit oQ3e bank — **only copy now** | `/Volumes/X10Pro/Flash4-1/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-oQ3e-mtp` | 331 GB |

### 17.2 What it should be worth

At a 36 GiB budget, per decoded token, carrying section 15's measured drive rate and compute:

    bytes/token   63.9 misses x 9.49 MiB                = 607 MiB   (was 988 MiB)
      at 6.7 GB/s                                       =  95 ms    (was 155 ms)
    compute, minus whatever 2-bit matmuls give back     < 134 ms
    experts per GiB of budget                           = 108       (was 69.3)

Two effects beyond the byte count itself: residency rises by 56 % at a fixed budget, which should cut the 63.9
misses that set the floor, and 2-bit `mx.quantized_matmul` micro-benchmarks at 1.7 us per expert row against
3-bit's 4.6 us, so some of the 18.6 ms the MoE spends per token should come back as well. **Decode should
become compute-bound for the first time in this project**, which is the regime every other lever in section
15.3 assumes.

The measurements that decide it, once the build finishes: `benchmarks/decode_throughput.py` against the
recorded 275 ms per token, `benchmarks/decode_anatomy.py` for the new blocked-time split, and
`nll_expert_precision.py --experts runtime` for the production-path quality number to compare against the
3-bit bank's 2.3080.

## 18. The bank, measured: 1.51x throughput, and decode is now compute-bound

The bank was built in 33 minutes, 40 shards, 142.4 GiB, at a steady 50 s and 7.7 experts per second per layer.
`--verify 12` read twelve sampled experts back through the real index and matched a fresh quantization of the
source byte for byte. Free space afterwards: 347 GiB.

`benchmarks/decode_throughput.py --prompt-tokens 512 --decode-tokens 64` at the 36 GiB budget the 275 ms
record was taken at:

| | 3-bit g64 (section 10) | 2-bit g128 | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| decode | 275 ms/token, 3.64 tok/s | **191 ms/token, 5.24 tok/s** | -31 %, 1.44x |
| expert hit rate | 73.4 % | 80.9 % | +7.5 points |
| misses per token | 63.9 | 45.8 | -28 % |
| bytes read per token | 1030 MiB | 486 MiB | -53 % |
| 512-token cold prefill | — | 16.4 s | — |

With `CACHALOT_PREDICT_TOPK=6`, which section 18.1 adopts, the same benchmark is **182.5 ms/token, 5.48
tok/s**: 1.51x the recorded 3.64.

Residency did what section 17.2 predicted: 108 experts per GiB instead of 69.3, so the miss count fell with
the byte count and the two multiply. 486 MiB per token against 1030 is better than the 64.3 % the format alone
buys.

`benchmarks/decode_anatomy.py` at the same budget, against section 10's numbers:

    decode 64 tokens: 12.22 s = 5.24 tok/s (191 ms/token)
      expert hit rate 80.9% | 45.8 misses/token | 486 MiB read/token
      expert wait 4.53 s =  70.8 ms/token (37.1% of decode)     was 151.3 ms/token (53.0%)
      rest        7.69 s = 120.1 ms/token (62.9% of decode)     was 134 ms/token
      drive busy 5.49 s of 12.22 s decode (45.0%); 2.03 reads in flight while busy
      blocked total           4.53 s =  70.8 ms/token
      a demand read in flight 4.36 s =  68.1 ms/token (96.2% of blocked) -- never predicted
      only a predicted read   0.10 s =   1.6 ms/token ( 2.2%) -- issued too late
      no read outstanding     0.07 s =   1.0 ms/token ( 1.5%) -- slots, eviction, locks

**Decode is compute-bound for the first time in this project.** Compute is 120.1 ms per token against 70.8 ms
of exposed wait, the drive is idle 55 % of the decode, and the byte floor is now 486 MiB at 6.7 GB/s = 73 ms,
well under compute. Compute itself also fell, 134 to 120 ms, which is the 2-bit `quantized_matmul` being
cheaper than the 3-bit one as the micro-benchmark predicted.

Per-read latency confirms there is still no fixed read tax: 3.28 ms for 9.49 MiB is 2.9 GB/s per stream,
exactly the 15.48 MiB at 5.28 ms the 3-bit bank gave. Reads got smaller, not slower, and while the drive is
busy the runtime still moves about 6 GB/s of the 6.7 available. What changed is that it is busy far less often.

### 18.1 Prediction width: the saddle moved

Section 13 closed the width sweep because width raised the drive floor faster than it closed the coverage gap.
That arithmetic was done at 15.48 MiB per expert. At 9.49 MiB the floor it raises stays under compute, so the
sweep was re-run. Every arm is a separate guarded run with `benchmarks/settle.sh` between, interleaved in both
directions:

| `CACHALOT_PREDICT_TOPK` | runs (ms/token) | mean |
|---|---|---|
| 3 (default) | 191, 190 | 190.5 |
| 4 | 188, 214 | 201.0 |
| 5 | 188, 185 | 186.5 |
| **6** | **183, 180** | **181.5** |
| 8 | 190, 184 | 187.0 |
| 12 | 219, 202 | 210.5 |

top-6's two runs are the two fastest of the twelve, and top-12 is clearly bad, which is the same shape as
section 13's table with the saddle moved outward. The within-arm spread reaches 7 % though (top-4 gave 188 and
214) against a 5 % effect, so the pair was re-run four more times each, still interleaved:

    top-3   193, 211, 192, 193      median 193
    top-6   182, 182, 184, 184      median 183

Six runs each, pooled: top-3 median 192.5 (spread 190 to 211), top-6 median 182.5 (spread 180 to 184).
**Every top-6 run is faster than every top-3 run**, the effect is 5.2 %, and top-6 is also the tighter arm.
`CACHALOT_PREDICT_TOPK` now defaults to 6 in `moe_layer_metal.py`, with both saddles recorded in the comment
there: the default is a function of expert size and has to be re-measured whenever the bank changes. Section
13's instruction not to re-run the width sweep applies to the 3-bit bank, and only to it.

### 18.2 Quality: the two paths disagree, and production is worse

`nll_expert_precision.py --experts runtime` serves the bank through the production kernels rather than the
dense reference math:

| bank | path | mean NLL | vs FP4 | perplexity | top-1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-bit g64, oQ3e | dense reference | 2.3398 | +0.0394 | 10.379 | 52.5 % |
| 3-bit g64, oQ3e | **production** | 2.3080 | +0.0076 | 10.054 | 53.1 % |
| 2-bit g128 | dense reference | 2.3234 | +0.0230 | 10.210 | 46.9 % |
| 2-bit g128 | **production** | 2.3337 | +0.0333 | 10.316 | 45.6 % |

**The ranking flips between the paths.** In dense math the 2-bit bank is 0.016 nats better than the 3-bit one;
through the production kernels it is 0.026 nats worse, and top-1 is 7.5 points lower. The 3-bit bank gains
0.032 nats moving from dense math to production while the 2-bit bank loses 0.010, so the dense gate could not
have predicted this and a production-path arm is now mandatory for any bank change. Both numbers are
deterministic on a fixed token sequence, so this is not noise; what it is not is a broad quality evaluation,
being 160 tokens of one text.

### 18.2.1 At 512 tokens the top-1 cost is real and the NLL cost is small

160 tokens is too few to read a 7-point top-1 difference off: the binomial standard deviation is 3.9 points
there. All three banks were therefore re-run on the production path at 512 tokens, same budget, same text,
where the standard deviation is 2.2 points:

| bank | mean NLL | perplexity | top-1 | vs the 3-bit bank |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-bit g64, oQ3e | 2.4997 | 12.178 | 50.8 % | — |
| 2-bit g128 | 2.5187 | 12.413 | 44.5 % | +0.0190 nats, +1.9 % ppl, -6.3 points |
| 2-bit g64 | 2.5327 | 12.587 | 46.5 % | +0.0330 nats, +3.4 % ppl, -4.3 points |

The absolute level is higher than the 160-token runs because the text continues into harder material; only
comparisons at equal length mean anything. Two things are now established rather than suggested. The **top-1
cost is real** — 6.3 points is 2.9 standard deviations — and the **NLL cost is small**, 2 to 3 % of
perplexity. And the two metrics disagree about which 2-bit bank is better: g128 wins on NLL by 0.014 nats,
g64 wins on top-1 by 2.0 points. Nothing in this session's evidence separates them confidently.

So the trade on offer is 1.51x decode throughput for about 2 % of perplexity and 6 points of top-1 next-token
agreement against the bank the project ran this morning. That is Hamed's call, not a number the gate can settle by itself, and the two
alternatives are measured and cheap to build:

- **2-bit g64** (10.55 MiB, 158.2 GiB): built and measured. 190 ms/token against g128's 182.5, production NLL
  2.3279 at 160 tokens and 2.5327 at 512, so it trades 4 % of speed and 16 GiB of disk for 2 points of top-1
  and loses 0.014 nats of NLL. It is on the internal SSD at
  `/Users/hamedprooshani/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-q2g64`.
- **our own 3-bit g64** (14.77 MiB, 221.5 GiB): 0.030 nats better than the oQ3e download in dense math at
  identical size, which is better quality than this morning with no speed change at all.

## 19. Levers, re-ranked after the bank

The arithmetic that drives this ranking, per decoded token at a 36 GiB budget with the 2-bit g128 bank and
top-6 prediction:

    bytes          45.8 misses x 9.49 MiB              = 486 MiB
      at 6.7 GB/s, what the drive gives                =  73 ms
    compute (attention, MoE, head, Python)             = 120 ms
    measured                                           = 182 ms

Compute is now the floor and the largest term, which inverts every ranking this document has carried. The
system spends 120 ms computing, 73 ms of drive time is hidden under it with room to spare, and the 62 ms
between 120 and 182 is exposed wait that better overlap could in principle remove.

1. **Compute.** Never the binding constraint before, so never attacked. 120 ms per token, and the only
   component measured at 2 bits is the whole: the 18.6 ms the MoE cost at 3 bits was `mx.quantized_matmul` on
   15.48 MiB experts, and the 2-bit kernel micro-benchmarks 2.7x faster per row. Re-run
   `benchmarks/profile_decode_components.py` and `benchmarks/profile_decode_gpu.py` against this bank before
   assuming where the 120 ms sits; the 2026-09-16 split (attention, MoE, head, Python) is stale.
2. **Read/compute overlap.** Ceiling is now compute, about 120 ms per token or 8.3 tok/s, and the gap to it is
   62 ms. 96.2 % of blocked time is still misses that were never predicted, and top-6 prediction has already
   taken the part of that a wider net can reach (section 18.1). Beyond it the mechanism problem of sections 10
   and 11 is unchanged: the runtime needs to know a layer's misses earlier than its own router can say.
3. **Quality recovery at 2 bits**, if the bank is adopted: +0.026 nats and 7.5 points of top-1 against the
   3-bit bank on the production path (section 18.2). It costs build time only, not runtime: a finer search
   grid, or a per-channel scale folded into the stored scales. `fit_search`'s grid is deliberately coarse for
   the gate's sake, and a bank is built once.
4. **Fewer bytes per expert.** Nearly exhausted inside MLX's affine format: `mx.quantized_matmul` accepts
   group sizes 32, 64 and 128 only, so 9.49 MiB is the floor at 2 bits, and 2-bit group 128 is already in use.
   Going lower needs custom kernels, which is a much larger piece of work than this session's was.
5. **Startup hotlist preload.** Unchanged, helps the first prompt of a session.
6. **Engram to internal SSD.** Still robustness only (section 2), but now affordable: deleting the internal
   oQ3e bank left 347 GiB free before the second bank was built.
7. **Closed.** Achieved bandwidth (section 15.2), page cache under a wired set (15), `F_RDAHEAD 0` (12),
   eviction policy (5), prediction lead time (11), and the width sweep **on the 3-bit bank only** (13) — on a
   smaller bank it reopened and paid 5.2 %.
